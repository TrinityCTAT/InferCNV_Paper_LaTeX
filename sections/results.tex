\subsection{Workflow on an example data set (Fig.~\ref{fig:1})}

Figure~\ref{fig:1} follows the 184-cell oligodendroglioma example distributed with the package (Smart-seq2; 42 non-malignant and 142 malignant cells from four tumours \citep{tirosh2016oligo}) through the main steps of the pipeline, run with the default settings, \texttt{denoise\ =\ TRUE} and \texttt{seed\ =\ 1}. The log-transformed expression shows no visible genomic structure. After subtraction of the reference and smoothing along chromosomes, the malignant cells are shifted upwards across the genome relative to the reference; per-cell centering removes this offset. After centering and the second subtraction of the reference, the expected loss of 1p and 19q in the malignant cells appears as contiguous blocks, together with tumour-specific changes such as loss of chromosomes 4 and 12 and gain of chromosome 7 in MGH36, while the reference cells remain close to neutral. Denoising clarifies the heatmap, and the HMM converts the residual signal into discrete, filtered state calls per subcluster: 1p and 19q are called at copy number factor 0.5 in all four tumours, and the reference cells have no calls.

\subsection{In-silico spike-in calibration (Fig.~\ref{fig:2})}

Figure~\ref{fig:2} illustrates the h-spike calibration on the full oligodendroglioma data set (4,043 malignant and 280 reference cells from six tumours \citep{tirosh2016oligo}). Counts for the simulated chromosomes are drawn from the mean--variance and mean--dropout trends of the data (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}a,b) and carried through the same processing (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}c). After processing, the residual levels of the simulated chromosomes are compressed relative to their nominal copy number factors (state means 0.48, 0.77, 1.00, 1.24, 1.48 and 2.02 for the 0-, 1-, 2-, 3-, 4- and $\geq$5-copy states, simulated at relative levels 0.01, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 3) and differ between data sets, even between the full data and the 184-cell example drawn from it (0.58, 0.84, 1.00, 1.16, 1.30 and 1.62; Fig.~\ref{fig:2}d), which is why parameters derived from the data themselves are used rather than fixed state means. The single-cell residuals of called events centre on the emission of the called state (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}e): the 1p deletion (called 1 copy) averages 0.76 against an emission mean of 0.77, the gain of chromosome 7 in MGH36 (3 copies) 1.25 against 1.24, and the higher-level 7p11--q11 segment around \textit{EGFR} in MGH36 (4 copies) 1.42 against 1.48, while the reference cells average 1.00; in the H3K27M glioma data set, 1q of BCH869, with five copies by DNA, is called $\geq$5 copies (1.52 against 1.61). Further events for each called state (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:S6}) show the same agreement for 1- to 4-copy calls, but also show that high-level amplifications are compressed into the open-ended top state, that uniform gains can be split between adjacent states (BCH869 1q is called as alternating 3-, 4- and $\geq$5-copy segments), and that the few 0-copy calls come from subclusters of one to five cells and are not convincing homozygous deletions. The standard deviation of the mean h-spike residual falls with the number of cells averaged with slope $-0.49$ to $-0.51$ on log--log scale, close to the $n^{-1/2}$ expected for independent cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:2}f). On the same malignant cells, the i3 and i6 HMMs agree on the direction of the call (loss, neutral or gain) for 91\% of gene--cell pairs, and i6 makes almost no calls that i3 does not; the differences are additional i3 calls, gains on 6.3\% and losses on 3.0\% of gene--cell pairs that i6 leaves neutral, mostly short segments and some whole arms (for example 8q and 20p; Fig.~\ref{fig:2}g).

\subsection{Application to tumours (Fig.~\ref{fig:3})}

inferCNV recovers the hallmark CNAs of tumours profiled with Smart-seq2 in the original studies of the method (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}; inferCNV\_examples data, run with the release and \texttt{seed\ =\ 1}). The 1p/19q co-deletion is called in all six oligodendrogliomas \citep{tirosh2016oligo}, and gain of chromosome 7 with loss of chromosome 10 in all six glioblastomas \citep{patel2014}. For the glioblastomas, which contain almost no non-malignant cells, the original study used a bulk normal-brain sample together with ten normal cells as the reference \citep{patel2014}; nine of these, oligodendrocytes, can be recognised in the data by their marker expression. With these nine cells as the reference (Fig.~\ref{fig:3}b), gains of chromosome 7 and losses of chromosome 10 were called for 97\% and 99.7\% of the corresponding gene $\times$ cell positions, and 25\% of positions on other chromosomes were non-neutral; with 1,026 GTEx bulk brain samples as the reference instead, the corresponding values were 93\%, 95\% and 45\%, and adding the GTEx samples to the nine cells gave intermediate background (32\%). A reference of matching normal cells from the same sample is therefore preferable, and bulk tissue profiles are a usable but noisier substitute. The melanomas \citep{tirosh2016melanoma} show diverse, tumour-specific profiles, and in the H3K27M gliomas \citep{filbin2018} gain of 1q is called in three of six tumours (BCH1126, BCH836 and BCH869), while MUV1 and MUV5 have few calls. In some oligodendroglioma and H3K27M glioma samples, subclusters within a tumour show distinct CNA patterns, as noted previously \citep{haas2021thesis}: in MGH97, 41\% of the cells carry loss of 4q and gain of 11q, and in BCH869, gains of 2q and 7q are called in 54\% and 47\% of the cells.

\placefloat{fig:3}

As an orthogonal check on the classification of cells as malignant, we compared inferCNV with detection of the SS18--SSX fusion transcript, the defining driver of synovial sarcoma, in three synovial sarcomas profiled by Smart-seq2, using the published counts and the per-cell fusion calls of the original study \citep{jerbyarnon2021}. For each patient, half of the published immune and stromal cells, one group per published cluster, served as the reference; the other half were held out and analysed together with the malignant cells, and each observed cell was classified from its CNV score and its correlation with the tumour's CNV profile, with thresholds set from the reference cells (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:S5}). Of the 479 observed cells with a detected SS18--SSX transcript, 474 (99.0\%) were classified as malignant (SS1, 204/208; SS13, 187/187; SS16, 83/84). Malignant cells without a detected fusion were classified as malignant at a similar rate (506/515, 98.3\%), consistent with incomplete detection of the fusion transcript rather than a distinct population. Of the held-out immune and stromal cells, 269 of 281 (95.7\%) were classified as non-malignant; most of the others lay near the thresholds or came from small clusters with little representation in the reference. Of the cells labelled malignant by the CNV analysis of the original study, 615 of 616 were classified as malignant; that label, however, covered only 42 of the 349 malignant cells of SS1, which accounts for most of the disagreement with it.

\subsection{Subclustering (Fig.~\ref{fig:subclusters})}

Because single-cell residuals are noisy, inferCNV runs the HMM on subclusters of cells by default (Fig.~\ref{fig:subclusters}). We compared the partition methods and HMM modes on the simulated four-subclone tumours (sim4; 230 tumour cells analysed as one annotation group; five seeds). The \texttt{random\_trees} method tests each split of the cell dendrogram against a permutation null of the maximum tree height (Fig.~\ref{fig:subclusters}a): in the Smart-seq2 example, the three splits that separate the subclones were significant (p < 0.01), while the dendrograms within subclones A and C were not (p = 0.50 and 0.82). The recursion therefore stopped at four subclusters that matched the subclones for 219 of 230 cells. Across seeds, \texttt{random\_trees} found four or five subclusters, with an ARI against the true subclones of 0.83 (Smart-seq2) and 0.87 (10x). Leiden subclustering, the default, produced 4--8 subclusters, splitting some subclones and merging others (ARI 0.64 and 0.86). The calls followed the partitions: \texttt{random\_trees} gave the highest cell $\times$ gene sensitivity and precision (Smart-seq2 0.97/0.99; 10x 0.99/0.97) against Leiden (0.95/0.96; 0.97/0.96). It took slightly longer at this size (11 and 6 min, against 9 and 5 min for Leiden). Leiden also subclusters the reference cells, and HMM calls in the smallest reference subclusters gave a non-neutral call rate of 0.6--1.5\% in the references, against $\leq$0.1\% with the other partitions. Per-chromosome Leiden subclustering did not improve the calls here (sensitivity 0.93 and 0.97). Without subclustering (\texttt{analysis\_mode = "samples"}), only the clonal CNAs were called (sensitivity 0.74 and 0.81). At the other extreme, per-cell HMM (\texttt{"cells"}) called many false CNAs (precision 0.38 and 0.58; non-neutral calls in 35\% and 20\% of reference gene positions) and took 5--12 times longer (110 and 49 min), mainly in the Bayesian model. Subcluster-level HMM therefore gave the best balance of resolution and noise. \texttt{random\_trees} is therefore preferable when resolving subclones is the aim; its run time at larger numbers of cells was not tested here, and these results come from one simulation design, in which subclones differ by whole-chromosome events.

\subsection{Accuracy against DNA-based copy number (Fig.~\ref{fig:4})}

We used samples and whole-exome copy number truth from the benchmark of \citet{schmid2025} and re-implemented its evaluation (Methods); the comparison with other callers is in that study. Two scores were averaged over malignant cells per genomic bin: the HMM states after Bayesian filtering (CNV score) and the final denoised residual expression (expression score).

\placefloat{fig:4}

For the 10x multiple myeloma sample MM199 (1,916 malignant cells, 245 reference B and NK/T cells), inferCNV's pseudobulk profile followed the exome copy number profile (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}). The CNV score had an AUC of 0.837 for gains and 0.779 for losses, a Pearson correlation with the truth of 0.581, and a maximal three-class F1 of 0.639 (gain sensitivity 0.562 and precision 0.730; loss sensitivity 0.467 and precision 0.683). The expression score gave similar AUCs (0.831 for gains, 0.786 for losses), the same maximal F1 (0.639) and a lower correlation (0.507). For context, Schmid et al.~reported for inferCNV v1.10 on this sample maximal F1 of 0.673 (CNV) and 0.664 (expression), gain AUCs of 0.837 and 0.849, loss AUCs of 0.782 and 0.801, and correlations of 0.612 and 0.585 \citep{schmid2025}. The values are close despite differences in input: Schmid et al.~reprocessed the reads and used 362 reference cells, whereas we used the deposited count matrix with a re-derived annotation and a newer gene annotation. The partial AUCs of the CNV score were higher than reported for v1.10 (0.796 against 0.700 for gains, 0.620 against 0.544 for losses), but the differences in input preclude attributing this to changes in the code.

The two basal cell carcinoma samples of the benchmark (BCC06 before and after treatment) are shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:S1}. Against their exome truth, both inferCNV scores were close to chance (AUC 0.57--0.62, maximal F1 0.41--0.42; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:S2}), as were the values reported for v1.10 (maximal F1 0.38--0.43) and for all callers in \citet{schmid2025}. The exome segmentation of these samples is highly fragmented (4,522 segments for BCC06, about half of them losses with small copy ratios) and labels about half of the genome as altered, whereas inferCNV shows a few clear arm-level events; BCC06 also has only 33 reference cells. These samples therefore measure the noise of the truth as much as the accuracy of the caller.

BCH869, an H3K27M glioma, contains only three non-malignant cells, so we ran inferCNV on its 489 malignant cells with the immune and oligodendrocyte cells of another tumour from the same study (BCH1126) as the reference, one group per cell type, using the published expression matrix of the study (Methods). We compared the result with the clone copy-number profiles that Filbin et al.\ derived from scRNA-seq and bulk whole-genome sequencing \citep{filbin2018}, as tabulated with per-cell clone labels by \citet{huang2024xclone} (Fig.~\ref{fig:4}b). Averaged over the cells of each clone, the HMM calls recovered all arm-level events of the three main clones (the shared gains of 1q, 2p and 17q and loss of chromosome 14, and the clone 1 gains of 2q, 3q, 7 and 19p), although the 17q gain was called in only part of the cells, and most events of the 7-cell clone 4. At the arm level, the CNV score had AUCs of 0.95 for gains and 1.00 for losses (maximal F1 0.83 and 1.00); at the gene level, 0.86 and 0.98 (F1 0.75 and 0.79). inferCNV also called a few regions absent from the truth (losses on 19p13 and 6q, and scattered focal changes), and the three non-malignant BCH869 cells carried scattered focal calls, consistent with residual differences between a reference drawn from another patient and the tumour. The Leiden subclusters separated clone 1 from the other cells (96\% agreement after assigning each subcluster to its majority clone) and isolated clone 4, but did not separate clones 2 and 3, which differ only by a gain of 2q, and split the large clones into several subclusters (adjusted Rand index 0.35).

\subsection{Accuracy on simulated data (Fig.~\ref{fig:5})}

Real data rarely provide cell-level truth, so we evaluated cell × gene accuracy on simulated tumours (Methods). Counts were simulated from normal cells of a Smart-seq2 data set (synovial sarcoma non-malignant cells \citep{jerbyarnon2021}) and a 10x data set, with CNAs covering whole chromosomes (0.5×, 1.5× and 2×) and parts of chromosomes (a 2× partial gain on chr2 and a near-complete loss on chr8), in one tumour population (sim1) or two to four subclones with shared and private events (sim2--sim4). inferCNV was run with the default Leiden subclustering, the i6 HMM and Bayesian filtering, five times with different random seeds.

\placefloat{fig:5}

Across scenarios, inferCNV detected 95--100\% of altered gene positions in tumour cells with the correct direction, and 92--97\% of its non-neutral calls were correct (Table 1, Fig.~\ref{fig:5}). Among altered genes, the exact i6 state was correct for 89--97\%. In the reference cells, 0.5--0.6\% (10x) and 1.1--1.5\% (Smart-seq2) of gene calls were non-neutral. Subclones were mostly separated by the Leiden subclustering, with subcluster purity falling from 1.00 for a single population to 0.91 (10x) and 0.84 (Smart-seq2) for four subclones of 30--100 cells that share most of their CNAs (sim4). The Bayesian filter mainly removed false calls: for Smart-seq2, it reduced the non-neutral call rate in reference cells from 1.5--2.0\% to 1.1--1.5\% and increased precision by about 0.01, at a cost of at most 0.01 in sensitivity; for 10x the changes were smaller.

With the three-state HMM, results on 10x data were similar to i6 in sensitivity (0.97--1.00) with slightly lower precision (0.90--0.95). On Smart-seq2 data i3 was more conservative, with lower sensitivity (0.89--0.96), higher precision (0.95--0.98) and fewer calls in reference cells (0.6--0.9\%). Simulated data are easier than real data. The simulator draws counts from the same mean--variance and dropout model that underlies the h-spike calibration, the simulated CNAs are large, and the simulations contain no batch effects, no cell-type-specific expression programmes among the malignant cells and no doublets. On real 10x data, expression-based calls are noticeably noisier than in these simulations. The simulations therefore show that the implementation behaves as intended, rather than estimating its accuracy in practice.

\subsection{Specificity and scale (Fig.~\ref{fig:6})}

To estimate the false-call rate on data without CNAs, we ran inferCNV on peripheral blood mononuclear cells from a healthy donor \citep{tenx2016pbmc3k, xclonedata} (2,625 cells in seven cell types; 2,286 genes after filtering), in four reference designs (Fig.~\ref{fig:6}a). When a random half of every cell type served as the reference for the other half, with one reference group per cell type, 8.7\% of genes $\times$ cells in the observations were in a non-neutral i6 state before and 5.9\% after Bayesian filtering (0--8.3\% by cell type). When the same reference cells were pooled into a single group, the rate rose to 16.7\% (15.0\% after filtering; 7--28\% by cell type), because the reference subtraction can only cancel cell-type-specific expression when the reference cell types are given as separate groups (see \emph{Expression signal} above). A random half of the CD4 T cells as the only reference gave 4.1\% (3.8\%) for the other CD4 T cells, but 10--31\% for other lymphocytes and 40--45\% for monocytes; with B cells as the reference, every other cell type had 16--45\%. These calls reflect cell-type expression programmes in gene-dense regions: chromosome arm 6p, and within it the MHC, was the most enriched region in the pooled and mismatched designs (2.1--3.8-fold its share of genes), followed by regions such as the interferon-induced IFITM cluster on 11p15 and 19p13. The Bayesian filter removed about a third of the calls with per-type reference groups, but only 5--10\% (relative) in the other designs, where the false calls form coherent multi-gene regions. The main safeguard against false calls is therefore a reference that covers the lineages of the cells of interest, given as separate groups per cell type.

\placefloat{fig:6}

Run time was the main practical criticism of inferCNV in \citet{schmid2025}. With 4 threads, the three tumour samples of Fig.~\ref{fig:4} ran in 12 min (BCC06post; 600 cells), 28 min (BCC06; 1,436 cells) and 50 min (MM199; 2,161 cells and 5,740 genes after filtering), with peak memory of 3.4, 9.6 and 14.8 GB. Most of the time was spent in the Bayesian model and its plots. On a 10x triple-negative breast cancer data set (TNBC1, 1,097 cells) run with 4 threads on a heavily loaded 8-core host, an expression-only run took 1.47 h, of which filtering, normalisation and smoothing took 10 min, Leiden subclustering 17 s, the HMM 6.5 min, the Bayesian model 57 min and the subsequent filtering 13 min; these times are upper bounds. In the simulations (200--330 cells, including 100 reference cells), runs took 3--8 min with the i6 HMM. On nested down-samples of a 10x breast cancer atlas \citep{wu2021breast} (GEO GSE176078; 500--20,000 cells, 80\% malignant cells from eight tumours and 20\% endothelial cells and fibroblasts as the reference; about 8,000 genes after filtering), run with 4 threads on lightly loaded cluster nodes, the default run (i6 with the Bayesian filter) took 17 min for 500 cells, 77 min for 2,500, 4.3 h for 10,000 and 9.4 h for 20,000, with peak memory of 4.7, 20, 66 and 117 GB (Fig.~\ref{fig:6}b--d). The Bayesian model accounted for 76--89\% of the time. Without it, the same runs took 4, 12, 33 and 61 min with 1.6, 3.6, 11 and 19 GB, scaling close to linearly with the number of cells; the i3 HMM was 4--46\% faster still (2--58 min). In these runs the HMM (33 min at 20,000 cells) and preprocessing (20 min) were the largest remaining steps, and Leiden subclustering took 2 min. Users with large data sets can skip the Bayesian model (\texttt{BayesMaxPNormal\ =\ 0}), use the i3 HMM, increase \texttt{num\_threads}, or subsample groups with \texttt{max\_cells\_per\_group}.

\subsection{Long-read single-cell data (Fig.~\ref{fig:7})}

Long-read single-cell RNA-seq, such as PacBio Kinnex (MAS-ISO-seq; \citealp{alkhafaji2023masseq}), sequences full-length cDNA from the same droplet libraries as short-read protocols and is used mainly to resolve transcript isoforms. Summed to genes, its counts can be analysed by inferCNV without change. We tested this on three data sets (Methods).

\placefloat{fig:7}

To measure false calls, we used Kinnex 10x 3$'$ data of PBMC from a healthy donor (9,936 cells; genes quantified with LRAA, \citealp{yu2026lraa}) in the two designs of Fig.~\ref{fig:6}a that use half of every cell type as the reference. After Bayesian filtering, 2.0\% of genes $\times$ cells in the observations were in a non-neutral state with one reference group per cell type and 3.5\% with the reference pooled into one group, against 5.9\% and 15.0\% for the short-read PBMC data of Fig.~\ref{fig:6}a (Fig.~\ref{fig:7}a). The two data sets come from different donors and differ in depth: about 6,000 genes passed the expression filter in the long-read data, against 2,286 in the short-read data. Calls spanning 40~Mb or more were concentrated in small subclusters of lower depth than the rest of their cell type (Spearman $\rho = -0.42$). The largest, an 85-cell CD4 T subcluster with gains on chromosomes 2q, 3 and 5, had half the median depth of the other CD4 T cells and a higher mitochondrial fraction, and so is more likely a group of low-quality cells than a clone.

LongBench \citep{you2025longbench} profiled the same 3,850 cells, from eight lung cancer cell lines pooled in one 10x 3$'$ library, with Kinnex, ONT and Illumina sequencing. We compared inferCNV's calls with the copy number of each line in the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE; \citealp{ghandi2019ccle}), averaged over each chromosome arm. With the Kinnex PBMCs above as an external reference, one group per cell type, the mean HMM state per arm correlated with CCLE copy number with $r = 0.81$ over all lines and $r = 0.61$--$0.89$ for individual lines (Fig.~\ref{fig:7}b). Without a reference, inferCNV measures each line against the mean of all cells, and its calls were compared with CCLE values centred in the same way. The correlations were then 0.80 for Kinnex, 0.78 for ONT and 0.78 for Illumina (0.67--0.89 for individual lines), so the three platforms gave nearly the same accuracy on the same cells (Fig.~\ref{fig:7}c).

Finally, we analysed patient-derived organoids of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) profiled by MAS-ISO-seq \citep{zajac2025ccrcc}, with the cells of a normal kidney organoid (390 cells) as the reference. Loss of chromosome arm 3p, the defining CNA of ccRCC, was called in every cell labelled malignant (ccRCC) by the original authors in three organoids, and in none of the cells of the normal organoid (Fig.~\ref{fig:7}d). It was also called in 97\% of the 975 cells labelled non-malignant in organoid ccRCC4 and in 88\% of the 157 such cells in ccRCC5, but not in organoid ccRCC3, all of whose cells were labelled non-malignant. Comparing each cell's mean 3p expression residual with the range of the normal organoid's cells gave similar fractions: 88--99.7\% in these groups, against 1\% in the normal organoid and 2\% in ccRCC3. These cells, labelled by expression, are therefore malignant cells in other transcriptional states. In ccRCC5, a gain of 5q, also common in ccRCC, was called in the cells labelled malignant but not in the other cells, which form a subclone with 3p loss alone.

\subsection{Spatial long-read data (Fig.~\ref{fig:8})}

inferCNV has been used to map CNAs in spot-based spatial transcriptomics \citep{erickson2022, wu2021liver}. We applied it to public Kinnex long-read data from Visium HD 3$'$ \citep{pacbio2026visiumhd} sections of a colon adenocarcinoma, a breast infiltrating ductal carcinoma and a tonsil, which served as a control without CNAs. Visium HD measures expression in 2-\textmu m bins, which hold only a few counts each in these data, so we summed them into 32-\textmu m spots and kept spots with at least 1,000 counts (median 2,155--2,975 counts per sample; Methods). In a first analysis that included spots with 300--1,000 counts, arm-level calls in these shallow spots followed their depth rather than tissue structure, in both the tonsil and the breast tumour. Spots were labelled by clustering and marker expression, and in the tumours the fibroblast spots served as the reference for the epithelial spots (Fig.~\ref{fig:8}a). Spots of immune cells did not form separate clusters at this resolution.

\placefloat{fig:8}

In the tonsil, with half of each cluster as the reference for the other half, 2.3\% of genes $\times$ spots were in a non-neutral state without Bayesian filtering, against 9.0\% in the colon and 22.7\% in the breast tumour epithelium (Fig.~\ref{fig:8}b, f). The genome-wide residual expression showed the main events of each tumour as blocks across most epithelial spots, and no comparable blocks in the tonsil (Fig.~\ref{fig:8}c). A subset of the colon reference spots carried the 8q and 13q gains, presumably because some 32-\textmu m spots at the tumour border contain epithelial cells as well as fibroblasts. In the colon tumour, gains of 8q and 13q, which are among the most frequent CNAs of colorectal cancer, covered most of the epithelium (59\% and 52\% of epithelial spots), together with a loss of 15q (51\%) (Fig.~\ref{fig:8}d). Gain of 21q was restricted to part of the tumour, and 8q was gained most strongly in a region where 21q was not, indicating spatially separated subclones. In the breast tumour, 13q was lost in 97\% of epithelial spots, and 8q and 1q were gained in 60\% and 58\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:8}e). A gain of 19p (47\% of spots) and a loss of 4q (25\%) coincided in a central region: 85\% of the spots with 4q loss also had the 19p gain, and these spots were not shallower than the others. With one fibroblast cluster instead of three as the reference, the non-neutral fraction in the breast epithelium fell from 22.7\% to 18.3\%, and the mean state per arm changed by at most 0.13 for these arms (for example, 13q $-0.96$ and $-0.91$). Without DNA copy number for these sections, the calls cannot be checked directly. Their agreement with the CNAs typical of each cancer, their spatial coherence and the low rate in the tonsil nevertheless indicate that inferCNV detects tumour CNAs and their subclonal structure in spatial long-read data.
