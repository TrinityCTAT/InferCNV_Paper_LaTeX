\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Somatic copy number alterations, from focal amplifications to gains and losses of whole chromosome arms, are among the most common genetic changes in cancer, and specific events characterise particular tumour types: co-deletion of 1p and 19q in oligodendroglioma and gain of chromosome 7 with loss of chromosome 10 in glioblastoma are two examples \citep{tirosh2016oligo, patel2014}. Copy number is usually measured from bulk DNA, which averages over all cells in a sample. Single-cell resolution adds two things that bulk measurements cannot provide. It identifies which cells in a tumour are malignant, which is not always evident from expression state alone, because malignant cells can resemble their non-malignant counterparts. It also resolves genetic subclones and relates them to the transcriptional states and microenvironmental context of the same cells.

Inference of CNAs from single-cell transcriptomes was introduced in the 2014 single-cell study of primary glioblastoma \citep{patel2014}. Because a gain or loss of a large genomic region shifts the expression of many neighbouring genes in the same direction, the average relative expression over windows of genes ordered by chromosomal position reveals large-scale CNAs, even though the expression of any single gene reflects copy number only weakly. In that study, relative expression averaged over large genomic regions separated malignant from non-malignant cells and recovered the chromosome 7 gain and chromosome 10 loss typical of glioblastoma \citep{patel2014}. The analysis was implemented in MATLAB by Itay Tirosh. The same approach was then used to identify malignant cells and their CNAs in single-cell atlases of melanoma \citep{tirosh2016melanoma}, oligodendroglioma \citep{tirosh2016oligo}, IDH-mutant glioma \citep{venteicher2017}, head and neck cancer \citep{puram2017}, H3K27M-mutant glioma \citep{filbin2018}, glioblastoma cell states \citep{neftel2019} and synovial sarcoma \citep{jerbyarnon2021}.

As single-cell profiling of tumours became widespread, there was a need for an open-source tool that would let any group apply the method routinely, without access to commercial software or to the original analysts. We developed inferCNV as an R package toward that goal, starting from Itay Tirosh's method, and distributed it through GitHub and later Bioconductor \citep{tickle2019infercnv, infercnvBioc}. Over time it was extended from a visualisation tool, from which CNAs were inferred by inspection of heatmaps, to one that also predicts discrete copy number states, assigns them confidence, and defines CNA-based subclusters of cells.

inferCNV has since been applied across many tumour types and platforms. Early named uses include melanoma \citep{jerbyarnon2018} and synovial sarcoma \citep{jerbyarnon2021}, and the package has been used with Smart-seq2 \citep{maynard2020}, droplet-based 10x Genomics \citep{ma2019, bi2021, steen2021}, Seq-Well \citep{raghavan2021} and single-nucleus RNA-seq \citep{slyper2020, terekhanova2023} data, in studies of uveal melanoma with orthogonal single-cell DNA validation \citep{durante2020}, high-grade serous ovarian cancer \citep{izar2020}, breast cancer \citep{wu2021breast}, small cell lung cancer \citep{chan2021}, and pan-cancer analyses of recurrent cell states \citep{barkley2022}. A full-text search of Europe PMC on 20 October 2026 returned 2,741 records mentioning ``inferCNV'', rising from 3 in 2018 to 758 in 2025; this is a lower bound, because only full texts held by Europe PMC are searched. More recently, the method has been carried into spatial transcriptomics. SpatialInferCNV applies inferCNV to spatially barcoded spots to reconstruct clonal CNAs in situ, including in histologically benign tissue \citep{erickson2022}, and has been used by others \citep{pei2025}; inferCNV has also been applied directly to spot-based \citep{wu2021liver, denisenko2024, lucas2024, mo2024, harwood2024} and spatially barcoded single-nucleus data \citep{russell2024}.

Independent benchmarks have included inferCNV. In the most extensive of these, \citet{schmid2025} compared six callers on 15 droplet datasets with DNA-based truth and found that inferCNV (v1.10) was among the methods with the highest maximal F1 score and ``performed relatively well in all evaluated categories'', while having the longest runtime and a higher sensitivity to gains than to losses. Other benchmarks found it among the best methods for identifying tumour subpopulations within a platform \citep{chen2025}, reliable with high precision except at very high tumour purity \citep{song2025}, and reliable when allelic information is unavailable \citep{chang2026preprint}; they also noted its sensitivity to batch and platform effects \citep{chen2025}. Despite this use, there has been no formal publication describing the algorithm and its implementation; users have cited the GitHub repository or the Bioconductor package \citep{tickle2019infercnv, infercnvBioc}.

This article provides that description. We describe the algorithm and its reference implementation, the final inferCNV release (Section 2 and Supplementary Methods), and illustrate its behaviour on simulated data with known CNAs and on tumour samples with DNA-based copy number measurements (Section 3). inferCNV is no longer funded or actively developed. This paper is a record of the method in its final form, written so that its behaviour is documented and so that it can be cited.
